**These are notes on The Status Game by Will Storr, read a second time. A place to hold the shape of the argument while it is fresh.**

## The game everyone plays

Storr says one thing drives us. Status. Not sex, not money, not power on their own, but the rank those things buy you in other people's eyes. We tell ourselves better stories. Career. Conviction. Fulfilment. Underneath sits a plainer need. You want to be seen as worth something by the people around you. Status is not a side effect of the group. It is what the group is built around.

## Three ways to win

There are three games. Dominance is the oldest. You win by force and fear, the strongman, the bully, the chief. Virtue is the moral game. You win by being good, or by looking good, and by punishing those who break the code. Success is the modern favourite. You win by doing, money, skill, smarts, work that gets noticed. Most people play more than one at once. The games bleed into each other.

## Why success games hurt

The success game looks fair. Effort in, reward out. That is the trap. When your worth is welded to what you achieve, your standing is only ever on loan. So you chase proof. You measure yourself against everyone. Failure stops being a lesson and starts to feel as if it could end you.

It shows up two ways. Some people overcompensate. They boast, they harden, they chase a perfect that does not exist. Others go the other way. The wins land, the evidence is right there, and still you do not believe your own success. ***Status is not something you chase. It is something other people hand you.*** That is why it never feels safe.

## When the threat comes from outside

Here it gets strange. ***The person above you may not fear your work at all. They fear your life.*** Money you don't need from them. A name you built elsewhere. A family, a calm, a thing they cannot rank.

The games are supposed to be separate. They are not. Success leaks. Someone who is winning outside the room unsettles the order inside it. Even if nothing formal changes, the fear creeps in. People might start asking who is really ahead. So the insecure answer is to shrink the other person's win. Call it luck. Call it unserious. Call it beside the point, here, where it counts. It looks like pettiness. It is usually defence.

> Note to self. This is Robert Sapolsky's ground in Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers. Watch baboons and stress runs downhill. The big male leans on the one below him, who leans on the one below that. He calls it displacement aggression. Humans do the same. The person caught in the middle, pressed from above and crowded from below, carries the most stress and passes it down the first chance they get. The kick-down is not a plan. It is a nervous system trying to steady itself. Read the two books side by side.

## You cannot just quit

So the obvious move is to leave. Change what you care about. Walk out of the room. Stop playing. In theory you can. In practice it barely works.

The reason is simple. You do not hold your own status; other people hold it for you. So when you leave, the group does not read it as a neutral choice. They read it as losing. Can't hack it. Couldn't cope. That story is not really about you. It protects the game. If leaving were allowed, the whole thing would look optional, and no one wants that.

## The tyranny of the cousins

Storr has a name for who enforces this. The tyranny of the cousins. It is not the king or the rival who keeps you in line. It is everyone else, the wide watching group. The cousins reward those who fit and punish those who drift. Try to step out and you meet it. You get made small, or left out, or nudged back in.

And the exit that does work gets absorbed too. Walk away well and you are suddenly wise, above it all, enlightened, which is just another rank. ***Escape does not end the game. It only moves it.*** Groups do not stop sorting people. It is what they do.

## What to keep

The games are not a sickness. They are not a moral failing. They are just human, and you are already playing. The harm does not come from playing. It comes from playing blind. Not seeing the game, letting insecurity drive, staking your whole self on one board.

Seeing it is the only real freedom on offer. Not freedom from status. That door is locked. Freedom to choose which games are worth your time, how much to care, and how much of you rides on the win. ***When you can see the game, you get to play it on purpose, or at least stop it from playing you.***
