**Notes on epistemic humility. The Dunning-Kruger effect read next to David Epstein's *Range*, and why breadth is the only honest cure for confidence.**

There is one line that holds the whole idea. ***You don't know what you don't know.*** It sounds like a riddle. It is closer to a map problem. What you have learned sits inside a boundary. Outside that boundary is everything else, and from the inside you cannot see how far it runs.

## What Dunning-Kruger actually says

The popular version is wrong. People repeat it as *stupid people think they are smart*. That is not the finding. The 1999 paper by Justin Kruger and David Dunning was narrower and stranger. In a given skill, the people who scored worst also guessed they had scored near the top. They lacked the very knowledge needed to judge their own answers. The same blind spot that made them fail made them unable to see the failure.

The mirror held too. Top performers slightly underrated themselves. They knew the task was hard, assumed others found it easy, and marked themselves down.

## The honest caveat

Here is where most write-ups stop, because the caveat is inconvenient. The classic curve may be partly a statistical ghost. Feed random noise into the same chart and a similar shape appears, an artifact of regression to the mean and of plotting a score against a guess built from that score. Later work argues the effect is smaller than the folklore, or in places just an accident of the math.

That does not erase the plain human observation. The unskilled often are confident. But it does mean the tidy graph should be held loosely. ***A finding that flatters our sense of other people's foolishness deserves more suspicion, not less.***

## Range, and the shape of the boundary

David Epstein's *Range* comes at the same wall from the other side. His argument is about breadth. Early, narrow specialization looks efficient and often is not. The generalist who samples widely before committing tends to do better in the long run, especially in what he calls wicked environments, where the rules are hidden and feedback lies.

The trap of deep expertise is that it builds a small, well-lit room and convinces you the room is the world. The specialist is not stupid. He is fluent inside his boundary and blind to its edge. Competence in one domain quietly poses as competence in general. This is Dunning-Kruger wearing a suit.

## The edge of the map

Put the two together and the lesson is not about intelligence. It is about the shape of the boundary between what you know and what you don't.

The least skilled cannot see the wall at all. The narrow expert sees only his own room's walls and mistakes them for the horizon. Breadth does something specific and uncomfortable: it shows you more walls. Every new field you touch reveals another vast area you will never master. ***Learning widely does not make you feel smarter. Done right, it makes you feel smaller, and more accurate.***

## What to keep

Sample before you specialize. Depth is worth earning, but earn it after you have seen enough of the terrain to choose where to dig.

Trust the quiet ones. The person hedging their claim has often mapped the edges of their knowledge. The person certain about everything usually has not.

Go looking for the walls. Comfort inside a boundary is not the same as knowing where it lies. The goal is not to feel expert. It is to keep an honest picture of how much sits outside the light.
