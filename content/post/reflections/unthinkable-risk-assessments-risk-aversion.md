Amanda Ripley's *The Unthinkable* set out to answer one question: what do people
actually do in a disaster? The answer is uncomfortable. We do not panic, and we do not
spring into action. Mostly, we stall. We deny, we deliberate, and only then — often too
late — do we move.

Reading it, I kept returning to Nassim Taleb's *Skin in the Game*, because the two books
describe the same failure from opposite ends. Ripley is looking at the acute moment: the
smoke in the room, the plane against the tower, the thicket of disbelief. Taleb is looking
at the tail: the rare, ruinous event that our intuitions are built to ignore. Both land on
the same conclusion — our sense of risk is poorly calibrated, and the calibration only
improves through proximity to consequence.

## We underreact to the danger in front of us

Ripley's most repeated finding is not hysteria but lethargy. A firefighter walks into a bar
full of smoke and finds customers still nursing their beers, declining to leave. Passengers
on a plane in distress laugh. People in the World Trade Center wanted, more than anything,
to stay at their desks.

The mechanism is ordinary. The brain runs on pattern-matching, and nearly every ominous
situation we have ever been in turned out to be nothing. So we file the new signal under
the old pattern. On top of that sits peer pressure: overreacting risks social
embarrassment, and no one wants to be the person who fled the bar over a little smoke. The
result, Ripley writes, is that "we err on the side of underreacting." The cost of that error
is delay, and delay in a real disaster can be fatal.

## We over-worry about the wrong things

The mirror image is stranger: the same people who won't leave a burning bar will lie awake
over a plane crash they will almost certainly never be in. Ripley admits she did
this herself — until she studied dozens of crashes and grew *less* anxious, not more. Having
glimpsed the worst case, she found the reality was usually less terrible than the fear.

This maps onto Taleb's distinction between noise and signal. The Code-Orange alerts on the
evening news trade in dread, and they aim it at vivid, low-probability events while
the mundane risks that actually kill people go unremarked. Calibration means reversing that:
paying attention to ruin, ignoring the noise. The point is not to fear more, but to fear
correctly.

Gerd Gigerenzer adds the hopeful corollary. In *Risk Savvy* he argues that most of our
statistical blundering is not hard-wired stupidity but a literacy problem: present a danger
as a natural frequency ("10 in 1,000") instead of a probability ("1%") and ordinary people
reason about it perfectly well. Miscalibration is partly a failure of presentation — which
means it can be taught out. That is the quiet optimism running under Ripley's own arc. She
became a better judge of risk not by acquiring courage but by acquiring information.

## Consequence, not expertise, produces the right response

The sharpest overlap concerns who acts. In almost every disaster Ripley studied, the rescues
were done first by ordinary people on the scene — lifting rubble with car jacks,
forcing air into voids with garden hoses. The search-and-rescue dogs arrived twenty-six hours
after the Halifax explosion; by then almost no one came out alive. After Hurricane Katrina,
Ripley says she learned more from people on street corners than from any homeland security
conference.

That is *Skin in the Game* stated plainly. The people who bear the immediate consequence act;
the detached institutional response is slow and arrives late. Expertise held at a distance
theorizes about risk. Proximity to consequence is what actually moves someone toward the
door — or toward the person trapped under the rubble.

## The takeaway

One practical lesson runs under both books: assume the modest signal is the start of
something, cut the denial-and-deliberation phase short, and trust the judgment of the person
with skin in the game over the alert on the screen. We are not built to assess risk well. We
can learn to.

## Highlights from the book

A curated selection of the highlights I underlined, grouped by theme.

### Disasters begin quietly

> Many of the worst disasters in history started quite modestly. (p. 6)

> "Life becomes like molten metal," he wrote. "Old customs crumble, and instability rules."
> — Samuel Prince, *Catastrophe and Social Change* (1920), the first systematic analysis of
> human behavior in a disaster.

> "This awful catastrophe is not the end but the beginning. History does not end so. It is
> the way its chapters open." — St. Augustine, quoted by Prince (p. 8)

> Knowledge will grow scientific only after the most faithful examination of many
> catastrophes. (p. 9)

### What survivors actually experience

> They performed better than they ever would have expected in some ways and much worse in
> others. (p. 11)

> Ironically, after writing a book about disasters, I feel less anxious overall, not more. I
> am a much better judge of risk now that I understand my own warped equation for dread. (p. 12)

> Survival often depends on the behavior of the passenger. These facts have been well known
> in the aviation industry for a long time. But unless people have been in a plane crash,
> most individuals have no idea. (p. 14)

### Denial, delay, and the survival arc

> This willful brand of denial can take the form of delay, which can be fatal. (p. 20)

> People in a disaster go through a series of phases along the survival arc. First a thicket
> of disbelief, followed by frantic deliberation, and, finally, action. (p. 30)

> "Actual human behavior in fires is somewhat different from the 'panic' scenario. What is
> regularly observed is a lethargic response." (p. 31)

> His station house would get dispatched to a bar; he would walk in and see smoke — and
> customers still sitting at the bar nursing their beers. He'd ask if they felt like
> evacuating. "They would say, 'No, we'll be just fine.'" — firefighter Jack Rowley (p. 31)

### Why we misread the signal

> The human brain works by identifying patterns. (p. 33)

> We inevitably see patterns where they don't exist. In other words, we are slow to
> recognize exceptions. There is also the peer-pressure factor... So we err on the side of
> underreacting. (p. 33)

### Who does the rescuing

> Before anyone else, regular people were on the scene saving one another... as in most
> disasters, the vast majority of rescues were done by ordinary folks. The search-and-rescue
> dogs did not arrive until twenty-six hours after the explosion. (pp. 13–14)

> In New Orleans, after Hurricane Katrina, I learned more from regular people on street
> corners than I learned covering any homeland security conference. (p. 12)
