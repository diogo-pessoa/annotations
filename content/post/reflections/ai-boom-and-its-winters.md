## The shape of the boom

The 2020s have produced the largest capital surge in the history of artificial intelligence. Estimates put the infrastructure buildout near one trillion dollars.[\[1\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) The backers are not universities or grant agencies. They are private equity, venture capital, and the largest technology corporations on the planet.

Earlier waves of AI optimism were funded by the public sector and academia. This one is funded by the market. The stakes are larger, the actors are fewer, and the money moves in a circle.

## The circle

{{< notice note "The circle is not OpenAI's alone" >}}
Anthropic runs the same play with a different partner. Through 2026 it pursued more than a gigawatt of self-operated data-center capacity, with Alphabet's Google guaranteeing its lease obligations and co-designing the chips it would run. That sits on top of a reported ~$40 billion Alphabet commitment in Anthropic.<a href="/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references">[4]</a> A backer that is also a chip supplier and a lease guarantor is the same loop, drawn between different names.
{{< /notice >}}

The defining feature of this boom is not the size of any single deal. It is how the deals connect.

In September 2025, Nvidia agreed to invest as much as $100 billion in OpenAI. OpenAI, in turn, committed to filling its new data centers with Nvidia chips.[\[1\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) The money flows out and returns as a purchase order. Critics named the shape immediately. It is *circular*.

The pattern repeated. OpenAI struck a partnership with AMD to deploy tens of billions of dollars of chips, and it is positioned to become one of AMD's largest shareholders.[\[1\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) It also committed roughly $300 billion to Oracle for cloud capacity. The same shape holds each time. Money leaves one balance sheet and returns as another firm's revenue. Each company's growth becomes collateral for the next company's growth.

This is the concern. When a customer is also an investor, and an investor is also a supplier, revenue and financing become hard to tell apart. Demand can look real while it is partly manufactured by the balance sheets that depend on it.

## Winters remembered

AI has been here before, twice.

The 1960s promised machine translation and general reasoning within a generation. The 1973 Lighthill report in Britain judged that the field had failed to deliver, and public funding collapsed. That was the first *AI winter*.

The 1980s promised expert systems and dedicated LISP machines. A commercial industry formed around them. It cratered when cheaper general-purpose computers undercut the hardware and the systems proved brittle outside narrow domains. That was the second winter.

Each cycle followed the same arc. Optimism arrived first. Media and money followed. Real-world results lagged. Confidence and capital withdrew together.

## What is different this time

The rhyme is clear. The differences are what make the current cycle harder to read.

| Period | Main backers | Hardware limit | Research focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1960s–70s | Academia, government, IBM | Severe | Theory, early applications |
| 1980s–90s | Academia, startups | High | Expert systems, LISP machines |
| 2010s | Academia, some VC | Major, until GPUs arrived | Neural networks |
| 2020s | Private equity, Big Tech | Largely overcome via AI chips | Scale, infrastructure |

Two shifts stand out.

The first is ownership. Prior cycles were driven by public research.[\[3\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) This one is industrialized and privatized. AI is now a product line inside the world's most valuable companies, not a discipline seeking a proof.

The second is hardware. In every earlier winter, compute was the ceiling. That ceiling has been bought down with custom silicon, GPUs, and data centers at national scale. The old constraint is gone. A new one has replaced it: **the financing itself**.

## Where the risk sits

Spending now runs far ahead of direct revenue. The gap is justified by a transformative narrative rather than by cash flow. That is a familiar posture. The dot-com era wore it too.

The circularity adds a second, sharper danger. Interlinked commitments mean the nodes are not independent. A failure at one major node does not stay local. It propagates. Valuations, supply agreements, and operational plans across the sector are wired to the same few names. Concentration that looks like strength on the way up becomes a transmission line on the way down.

Not every reading treats the buildout as hollow. Analysts including Dan Ives and Gil Luria argue that this cycle is separated from earlier manias by two concrete facts. The spending buys real, physical infrastructure. The largest AI services already earn genuine consumption revenue. That is the case against a simple dot-com replay. The rebuttal does not remove the danger. It relocates it. When capital, suppliers, and customers concentrate into the same handful of names, the whole market begins to behave as a single trade.[\[5\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) Diversification turns into an illusion. One correction moves everything at once.

## What history rhymes with

The boom-and-retrenchment arc is settled precedent.[\[2\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) What is unsettled is whether scale changes the ending.

Earlier AI was a small academic pursuit, vulnerable to a single funding decision but cheap to abandon. The present field is an industry. That cuts both ways. An industry is more durable, with more capital and more customers holding it up. It is also more exposed. A discipline can go quiet. An industry this wired can fail loudly.

## What to watch

History does not settle whether this ends in a breakthrough or a bust. It only names the signals worth tracking.

- **Monetization.** The demand is not imaginary. AI revenue climbed from roughly zero two years ago to a combined run rate now reported in the $100–120 billion range, with the two largest labs supplying most of it.[\[6\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) Two cautions sit under that headline. These are run-rate estimates, not booked annual revenue. And the number does not separate durable demand from circular demand. Revenue from end customers survives a downturn. Revenue that loops between AI companies does not.
- **Growth beyond the circle.** For now, demand is real and widening. Prices are falling fast. A price war across 2025 and 2026 cut the cost of comparable model intelligence by as much as 90 percent, driven by cheap Chinese open-source models that forced the largest US labs to slash their own rates.[\[7\]](/post/reflections/ai-boom-and-its-winters/#references) Cheaper inference tends to expand a market, not shrink it. The buildout is framed by some as the early stage of a decade-long cycle, even as others already call it a bust. Both readings can hold at once. Falling prices grow usage and compress margins in the same motion.
- **Regulation and investment discipline.** This is the murkiest signal. Calls for regulation are not neutral. An incumbent that asks to be regulated may be asking to raise the ladder behind it. Safety warnings blur with competitive strategy when a lab frames a rival-class model as a national-security risk. And the Western debate is only half the map. China governs AI, data, and privacy on entirely different terms, which caps how far any single jurisdiction can steer the technology.

None of this is a forecast. History is context, not prophecy. The winters are worth remembering precisely because this cycle differs from them in ways that matter. The infrastructure is real. The revenue is real. The market has so far absorbed each shock — a price war, a cheaper rival, a circular-deal scare — and kept building. The circularity is a genuine fault line, and it deserves watching. The likelier path, though, is adaptation rather than collapse. The three signals above are how to tell the two apart in time.
