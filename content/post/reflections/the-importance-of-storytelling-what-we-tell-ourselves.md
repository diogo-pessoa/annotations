Humans are not purely rational. We like to believe we decide from facts, evidence, and logic. Most of the time we are guided by something older and more intuitive: stories.

We tell stories to make sense of the world, to explain who we are, and to justify why things happen the way they do. Nations are built on stories. Companies survive on them. Relationships thrive or collapse depending on the stories two people tell about each other. And the inner life is shaped by the stories a person tells themselves.

Storytelling is not only a creative or cultural act. It is a cognitive tool. It compresses complexity into meaning. Seen from a distance, it is also a system: stories do not merely describe reality, they shape perception, guide behaviour, and influence outcomes, which then feed back into the story.

## Storytelling as a sense-making tool

Reality is messy, ambiguous, and often overwhelming. A story acts as a compression algorithm. It filters chaos into something coherent enough to understand and act on.

A story supplies three things:

* **Causality** — this happened *because* of that.
* **Continuity** — a past, a present, and a future.
* **Identity** — a protagonist, an antagonist, a role.

Without stories, events feel random. With them, events feel purposeful, even when the purpose is assigned after the fact. This is why the same set of facts can produce opposite interpretations depending on the story wrapped around them. Facts alone rarely change minds. Stories give facts emotional weight and direction.

## Shared stories and collective reality

At the level of a society, storytelling lets large groups cooperate at scale. Money, laws, borders, corporations, and even human rights exist because enough people agree on the stories behind them. Yuval Noah Harari calls these shared fictions, and argues they are exactly what let humans organise beyond the small band.

These shared narratives are powerful precisely because they feel real, even though they are abstract. Once enough people believe a story, it begins to shape behaviour, incentives, and outcomes, and it reinforces itself. We believe a story. We act according to it. The world responds. The story feels confirmed.

The danger is not that stories are false. It is that we forget they are stories at all.

## The stories we tell ourselves

Collective stories shape societies. Personal stories shape lives.

Each of us carries an internal narrative that answers quiet questions. *Who am I? What kind of person am I? What do my past experiences say about my future?* These stories are rarely spoken aloud, but they steer behaviour. Someone who believes "I always mess things up" reads neutral outcomes as failures, and each reading files away one more piece of evidence for the story.

In this sense the story becomes self-fulfilling. What makes it subtle is that we mistake the narrative for objective truth rather than for one interpretation among several.

## Feedback loops: how stories reinforce themselves

Once a story is in place, it drives behaviour. That behaviour changes the environment. The environment feeds back into the story. A simple loop:

1. **Story** — "I'm not good at speaking up."
2. **Behaviour** — voicing fewer opinions.
3. **Outcome** — ideas go unheard.
4. **Interpretation** — "See? I don't have much to contribute."
5. **Reinforcement** — the story grows stronger.

This is a positive feedback loop. Not positive in value, but in amplification. The loop entrenches itself over time and grows harder to break, even when the outcome is bad. Breaking it means intervening at the level of the story, not only the behaviour.

The same dynamics scale up. Shared narratives shape institutions, incentives, and norms, and once embedded they produce the very behaviour that makes the story look objectively true. "This is how things are done here." "People like us don't succeed in that field." "Growth must always continue." Systems optimise around the story, so challenging it feels disruptive, not because it is false but because it threatens the stability of the system.

## Meaning and narrative coherence

Happiness is often framed as pleasure or success. Derren Brown, in *Happy*, makes the case that it has more to do with meaning than with either, and that meaning looks a lot like narrative coherence: the sense that a life adds up as a story.

Even hardship becomes bearable when it fits a larger narrative. *This is temporary. This is shaping me. This matters for what comes next.* Comfort without meaning, by contrast, can feel hollow when it lacks direction. This is why people endure difficulty for causes, identities, or futures they believe in. The story gives the suffering a place to sit.

From a systems view, meaning is a stabiliser. It reduces uncertainty and keeps the system running under stress, which is also why people cling to a narrative even when the evidence cuts against it. A flawed story that holds coherence is often preferred over honest uncertainty. Meaning keeps the system stable. It is not the same as truth.

## Editing the narrative

If stories shape perception and behaviour, then examining them is a form of agency. This does not mean inventing comforting lies. It means noticing the recurring narratives, questioning where they came from, separating events from interpretation, and letting the stories evolve.

The most effective place to intervene is not the event but the frame around it. A more useful set of questions than "What happened?" is:

* *What story am I using to explain this?*
* *What behaviour does this story produce?*
* *What does it reinforce over time?*

And the sharpest question is not "Is this story true?" but "Is this story useful?" Some stories protect us. Others limit us. Many were written by earlier versions of ourselves under constraints that no longer apply. Small shifts in the narrative can produce large changes downstream, because they interrupt the loop.

## What we choose to keep telling

Storytelling is unavoidable. We cannot opt out of it. We can only become more conscious participants.

Whether at the scale of civilisations or inner dialogue, stories are how humans turn experience into meaning. They are powerful, dangerous, necessary, and deeply human. Once seen as systems rather than passive descriptions, stories become active components we can examine, test, and occasionally rewrite.

What matters most may not be the stories we inherit, but the ones we choose to keep telling.
