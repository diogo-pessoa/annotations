Kurt Vonnegut once listed eight rules for writing a short story. They read like craft advice for novelists. Read again, they are something larger: instructions for being understood by another human being.

Every one of them starts from the same place. The reader is a stranger who owes you nothing, running their own life, lending you their attention for a few minutes. The rules are what you owe them back.

## Vonnegut's rules

1. **Don't waste the reader's time.** Use a stranger's time so that they will not feel it was wasted.
2. **Give the reader somebody to root for.** At least one. Loyalty is singular before it is plural.
3. **Every character should want something,** even if it is only a glass of water. Desire is the engine. A character who wants nothing is furniture.
4. **Every sentence must do one of two things** — reveal character or advance the action. If it does neither, cut it.
5. **Start as close to the end as possible.** Enter late, leave early. Skip the runway.
6. **Be a sadist.** No matter how sweet the leads, make awful things happen to them, so the reader sees what they are made of. Resilience is only visible under pressure.
7. **Write to please just one person.** Open a window for one reader, not a wall for everyone. Writing for everyone is writing for no one.
8. **Give the reader as much information as possible, as soon as possible.** No withholding for cheap suspense. The reader should be able to finish the story themselves if the writer drops dead.

Vonnegut added a caveat: the greatest writers break all of these. Flannery O'Connor broke almost all of them — every one but the first — and was still great. The rules are a floor, not a ceiling.

There is a ninth, not his:

9. **Give readers what they need.** Not just information — the point. Communicate well. Drive the idea home, and hand it over in a form they can actually use.

That last one is the hinge. It is where the craft walks out of fiction and into every conversation, every message, every time you need someone else to act on what you know.

## The same rules, without the story

Strip away the word "character" and the rules stop being about fiction.

*Don't waste their time.* Get to it. *Every part earns its place.* Every part of what you say should reveal something or move things forward. *Give the information as soon as possible.* Front-load what they need to follow you, instead of hoarding it for a reveal that serves you and not them.

These are not three techniques. They are one instinct wearing three coats: **respect the reader by being clear.**

The instinct is easy to state and hard to keep. It is more flattering to be mysterious, to hold the good part back, to write the thing that sounds impressive rather than the thing that lands. Every one of those temptations spends the reader's time to buy the writer's comfort. Vonnegut's rules are a standing refusal to make that trade.

## A story is a lens

The rules work because of something underneath them. A story is not a description of reality. It is a lens for it — and so is the private narrative each person carries.

We are not purely rational. We like to believe we decide from facts, but most of the time we are guided by the stories we use to explain the world and ourselves. The same set of facts produces opposite conclusions depending on the story wrapped around them. Facts rarely change minds on their own. Stories give facts weight and direction. (This is a thread from an [earlier reflection](/post/reflections/the-importance-of-storytelling-what-we-tell-ourselves/) — that the stories we tell ourselves quietly shape what we notice, fear, and want.)

Rule 3 is where fiction and life touch most directly. *Every character wants something, even if it is only a glass of water.* The glass of water is never really the point. It is the visible edge of a deeper want. The craft of giving a character a small, concrete desire is the same craft as **asking why** in real life — getting beneath the stated want to the real driver.

Feynman liked to point out how quickly "why" runs away from you. Ask why two magnets push apart and, a few honest "why"s later, you are standing at the edge of physics itself, unable to answer without rebuilding the whole world from scratch. The answer depends entirely on the framework you agree to stand inside. A good question, like a good scene, chooses that frame on purpose.

## Reality lenses

Follow the thread and it converges on a single point: **how people perceive the world.**

Every reader, every person on the other side of a message is running their own story. What you send does not land in a vacuum. It lands inside their narrative, and their lens bends it before they have finished the sentence.

This reframes the two hardest rules.

*Write to please just one person* is not about narrowing the audience. It is about **knowing whose lens you are writing into.** A message aimed at everyone assumes no lens at all, which is why it refracts into nothing.

*Give readers what they need* is not only about volume of information. It is about form. The same truth, handed over in a shape the other person's lens can accept, is received; handed over in a shape it rejects, it bounces off, however true it was.

So the good communicator — writer, engineer, friend — is not the one with the cleanest facts. It is the one **sensitive to the different realities on the other side.** They speak to the lens, not past it, because to that person the lens is the only real thing in the room.

## What the rules are really for

Vonnegut wrote rules for fiction and, without saying so, wrote rules for empathy. The reader is a person, with a story of their own, lending you their attention — and the whole craft is what you do with that trust.
