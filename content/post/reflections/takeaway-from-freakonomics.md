Freakonomics is not really a book about economics. It is a book about incentives, and about how often the story everyone agrees on turns out to be wrong.

## Economics is the study of incentives

Levitt and Dubner keep returning to one idea: people respond to incentives, and the incentives that drive behavior are not always the ones we assume. There are economic incentives, social incentives, and moral incentives, and they often pull in different directions. To understand why something happens, follow the incentives rather than the stated goals.

This maps cleanly onto why companies stall and projects slip. It is rarely incompetence. It is usually misaligned incentives.

- Executives optimize for short term results that lift the end of year bonus.
- Middle management optimizes for keeping the job and staying on the executive's good side, while making sure their people look visible and hit the expected numbers.
- Employees optimize for income, but they are not paid for output. They are paid to show up. Stock awards vest on time, not on performance, so the longer you stay the more you accrue. Looking busy and hitting the expected numbers becomes the rational strategy.

Nobody in that chain is acting against their own interest. That is the uncomfortable part. The system produces the outcome it rewards.

## The story everyone agrees on is often wrong

The other habit the book teaches is distrust of conventional wisdom. Experts hold better information than the people they advise, and that gap is an incentive in itself. A real estate agent sells your house and their own house differently, because the information asymmetry pays them to. The lesson is to separate correlation from causation, and to ask who benefits from the accepted explanation.

## Only trade-offs

Reading Freakonomics sent me toward Thomas Sowell and Basic Economics, which sharpened the same instinct from the other side. Sowell's line that there are no solutions, only trade-offs, is the cleaner statement of what Freakonomics shows through its stories. Price controls and rent control are his favorite example: cap the price and you do not remove the cost, you move it somewhere less visible, into shortages, worse quality, and black markets. The intent of a policy tells you nothing about its result. The incentives do.

That is the takeaway I kept. Do not read the goal. Read the incentive, and read the trade-off.
