I picked up Timothy Snyder's *On Tyranny* expecting a book about dictators. The line that stayed with me isn't about them at all: *"Most of the power of authoritarianism is freely given."* Snyder's first lesson is *do not obey in advance*. The throne, it turns out, is usually assembled by the people who will kneel before it.

La Boétie got there four centuries earlier. In 1576 he asked how it happens that "so many men, so many villages, so many cities, so many nations, sometimes suffer under a single tyrant who has no other power than the power they give him." His answer was almost anticlimactic: the tyrant is not toppled by force but by withdrawal. Stop serving and he falls on his own. Tyranny is a transaction before it is a position.

## You don't need a dictator

Once you see tyranny as a transaction rather than a title, scale stops being the point. A head of state has the army; a team lead has a calendar invite — but the difference is only the coercion, and coercion turns out not to be the essential ingredient. Strip it away and the tyranny doesn't dissolve; it just stands there in the open, the transaction laid bare. The office version is the clearer one, because there's nothing else propping it up. You do not need a dictator to live under a dictatorship. You need only a small arena — a team, a household, a committee — and one person who has learned that others will hand over their judgment when the price of keeping it feels too high.

I've watched it happen in rooms with no villains in them.

The most effective petty tyrants I've known did not rule from strength. They ruled from apparent weakness. The therapist Steven Wineman gave this its name — *power-under*: "Someone acting from an internal state of sheer powerlessness can have an enormously powerful impact on anyone in their path." The colleague forever on the edge of being overwhelmed; the family member whose moods are a weather system everyone else plans around. They are not powerless. The felt powerlessness *is* the instrument.

Stephen Karpman drew the mechanism as a triangle — Persecutor, Victim, Rescuer, three chairs everyone rotates through — and the trick of it is that whichever chair you start in, the victim's is where the power sits. The Victim blames and recruits; the estate gets built out of everyone else's guilt.

## Why you can't argue back

What makes this so hard to counter is that the frame is set before you open your mouth. Push back on someone who rules from grievance and you find you've been recast as the aggressor. The psychologist Jennifer Freyd named the move DARVO — Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender. The stronger you objectively are, the better it works: your strength becomes the evidence against you. A manager who raises a fair concern with a "struggling" report can be made to look like a bully for raising it at all.

There's a status logic underneath this. Victimhood is not only a wound; it is a move — and in a culture that hands status to the wronged, the wound becomes a claim. As Danielle Carr puts it, "our trauma is the guarantor of what we believe we are owed." (I've pulled on this thread before, in [Revisiting *The Status Game* by Will Storr](/post/reflections/reflections_on_status_game/).)

## The tyranny of the "No"

Then there is the subtlest form of small power: the *No*. Telling other people what they may not do almost always arrives wrapped as a gift. *You can't do that — it's for your own good.* C.S. Lewis saw the trap: "Of all tyrannies, a tyranny sincerely exercised for the good of its victims may be the most oppressive… those who torment us for our own good will torment us without end, for they do so with the approval of their own conscience." The robber baron eventually sleeps. The moral busybody never does, because in his own mind he isn't sinning — he's helping.

At work this is the most common tyranny there is, precisely because it is the easiest to disguise. Almost any refusal can be dressed as protecting the team. *We can't try that — too risky for the group. We shouldn't let them own that — it's for their own development.* This is the Rescuer's chair, and the rescuer's private reward is the rescue itself: there's no cheaper way to feel important than to be someone's savior — and the saving tends to leave the saved smaller than it found them. The No that protects you is often the No that keeps you small. I've followed this to its far end — refusal as governance at the scale of information itself — in [LLMs and the Quiet Gatekeepers](/post/reflections/llms-paternalistic-society-protection/). The lever is identical whether it's an operating system or a colleague: whoever gets to say *no, for your own good* quietly rules.

There is a way out of the triangle, and at the personal level it isn't more power over others, nor the practiced power-under of the victim, but what Wineman calls *power-with*: the Victim becomes a maker, the Persecutor a challenger, the Rescuer a coach. Underneath sits Alfred Adler's older observation that what we're all really chasing is belonging and significance — the sense that we matter. Tyranny, macro or micro, is what happens when someone tries to secure their own significance by shrinking everyone else's. But willpower is a fragile guardrail, and the deeper protection isn't personal at all.

## No throne to seize

So what actually protects a group — not the saintly individual, but the group? Not a stronger, fairer ruler; that's just a better-upholstered throne. The protection is structural, and Nassim Taleb drew its shape in *Antifragile*.

His example is Switzerland: no dominant central government, a bottom-up federation of small cantons and municipalities that, in his phrase, "fight each other." The Swiss, he notes, can barely name their own president — and that is exactly the point. Fragility scales with size. A large centralized structure hides its risks until it breaks all at once; small independent units take their shocks locally, and sometimes grow stronger for them. A monolith has a single point of failure: capture the center and you own the whole thing.

Every tyrant in this essay is a single-point-of-failure attack. The colleague who rules by grievance, the Rescuer who rules by the protective No, the reverser who flips victim and offender — each one works by capturing a center: the group's attention, its guilt, its permission to act. A small, genuinely independent team is antifragile against all of them in a way a large, dependent one can never be, because there is no throne — no single seat whose occupant everyone else must appease. The checks run sideways: members keep each other honest, and no one person's fear, or virtue, gets to become the group's operating system.

Which is why the surest sign that a throne is going up is the No offered as help. In a healthy small team, *"we can't — for the group's own good"* is a claim that has to survive everyone else's judgment. In a fragile one, it's the spell that ends the conversation.

Snyder's first lesson was *do not obey in advance*. The quieter version, for the rooms most of us actually live in: don't hand over your judgment in advance — and be most careful with the person who offers to hold it for your own good.
