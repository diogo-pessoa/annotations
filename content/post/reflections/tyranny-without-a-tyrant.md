I picked up Timothy Snyder's *On Tyranny* expecting a book about dictators. What I found was a book about the rest of us. Lesson by lesson, its argument is the same: power is given, not taken. The line that stayed with me is the plainest of them. *"Most of the power of authoritarianism is freely given."* Snyder's first lesson is the response. *Do not obey in advance.*

La Boétie saw this four centuries earlier. In 1576 he asked how it happens that *"so many men, so many villages, so many cities, so many nations, sometimes suffer under a single tyrant who has no other power than the power they give him."* His answer was almost anticlimactic. Stop serving, and the tyrant falls on his own. Tyranny is a transaction before it is a position.

## You don't need a dictator

Once you see tyranny as a transaction rather than a title, scale stops being the point. A head of state has the army. A team lead has a calendar invite. The only difference is the coercion, and coercion is not the essential ingredient. Strip it away and the tyranny does not dissolve. It stands in the open, the transaction laid bare. The office version is the clearer one, because nothing else props it up. You do not need a dictator to live under a dictatorship. You need a small arena: a team, a household, a committee. And you need one person who has learned that others will hand over their judgment when the price of keeping it feels too high.

I have watched it happen in rooms with no villains in them.

The most effective petty tyrants I have known did not rule from strength. They ruled from weakness, or the look of it. The therapist Steven Wineman gave this a name. He called it *power-under*. *"Someone acting from an internal state of sheer powerlessness can have an enormously powerful impact on anyone in their path."* Think of the colleague always on the edge of being overwhelmed. Think of the family member whose moods are a weather system everyone else plans around. They are not powerless. The felt powerlessness is the instrument.

Stephen Karpman drew the pattern as a triangle. Persecutor, Victim, Rescuer. Three chairs everyone rotates through. The trick is that wherever you start from, the victim's chair is where the power sits. The Victim blames, and the Victim recruits. The throne gets built out of everyone else's guilt.

## Why you can't argue back

The frame is set before you open your mouth. Push back on someone who rules from grievance, and you find you have been recast as the aggressor. The psychologist Jennifer Freyd named the move DARVO: Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender. The stronger you are, the better it works. Your strength becomes the evidence against you. A manager raises a fair concern with a struggling report, and he is the one made to look like a bully.

There is a status logic underneath this. Victimhood is not only a wound. It is a move. In a culture that hands status to the wronged, the wound becomes a claim. I have pulled on this thread before, in [Revisiting *The Status Game* by Will Storr](/post/reflections/reflections_on_status_game/).

## The tyranny of the "No"

Then there is the subtlest form of small power. The *No*. Telling other people what they may not do almost always arrives wrapped as a gift. *You can't do that. It's for your own good.* C.S. Lewis saw the trap. *"Of all tyrannies, a tyranny sincerely exercised for the good of its victims may be the most oppressive… those who torment us for our own good will torment us without end, for they do so with the approval of their own conscience."* The robber baron eventually sleeps. The moral busybody never does. In his own mind he is not sinning. He is helping.

At work this is the most common tyranny there is, because it is the easiest to disguise. Almost any refusal can be dressed as protecting the team. *We can't try that. Too risky for the group. We shouldn't let them own that. It's for their own good.* This is the Rescuer's chair. The rescuer's real reward is the rescue itself. There is no cheaper way to feel important than to be someone's savior, and the saving tends to leave the saved smaller than they were. The No that protects you is often the No that keeps you small. I have followed this to its far end. Refusal as governance, at the scale of information itself. That is [LLMs and the Quiet Gatekeepers](/post/reflections/llms-paternalistic-society-protection/). The lever is the same whether it is an operating system or a colleague. Whoever gets to say *no, for your own good* quietly rules.

There is a way out of the triangle. At the personal level it is not more power over others, and it is not the practiced power-under of the victim. Wineman calls it *power-with*. The Victim becomes a maker. The Persecutor becomes a challenger. The Rescuer becomes a coach. Underneath sits an older observation from Alfred Adler. What we are all chasing is belonging and significance, the sense that we matter. Tyranny, large or small, is what happens when someone secures their own significance by shrinking everyone else's. But willpower is a fragile guardrail. The deeper protection is not personal at all.

## No throne to seize

So what protects a group? Not a saint in the chair. Not a stronger, fairer ruler, because that is only a better throne. The protection is structural, and Nassim Taleb drew its shape in *Antifragile*.

His example is Switzerland. There is no dominant central government. It is a federation of small cantons and municipalities, built from the bottom up, that in his phrase *"fight each other."* The Swiss can barely name their own president, and that is the point. Fragility scales with size. A large central structure hides its risks until it breaks all at once. Small independent units take their shocks locally, and sometimes grow stronger for them. A monolith has one point of failure. Capture the center, and you own the whole thing.

Every tyrant in this essay works the same way: the colleague who rules by grievance, the Rescuer who rules by the protective No, the reverser who flips victim and offender. Each one captures a center. The group's attention, its guilt, its permission to act. A small, genuinely independent team is antifragile against all of them, in a way a large and dependent one can never be. There is no throne. There is no single seat whose occupant everyone else must appease. The checks run sideways. Members keep each other honest. No one person's fear, or virtue, becomes the group's operating system.

This is why the surest sign that a throne is going up is the *"No"* offered as help. In a healthy small team, *"we can't, for the group's own good"* is a claim that has to survive everyone else's judgment. In a fragile one, it is the spell that ends the conversation.

Snyder's first lesson was *do not obey in advance*. The quieter version, for the rooms most of us actually live in, is simple. Do not hand over your judgment in advance. And be careful with the person who offers to hold it for your own good.
