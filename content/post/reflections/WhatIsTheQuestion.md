**Notes on *What is the Question?* by Itai Yanai and Martin Lercher, and why the hardest, most valuable part of any inquiry is not the answer but the question that reframes it.**

The question is often worth more than the answer. Facts matter, but what shapes our understanding of the world is the framework we use to interrogate it. Yanai and Lercher build a whole account of scientific discovery on that claim, and Richard Feynman reached the same place from the other direction: change the question, and you change what counts as an answer.

***The right question does more than seek an answer. It decides which answers are even possible.***

## The Infinite Regress of "Why"

Feynman liked to show how quickly "why" runs away from you. Ask why Aunt Minnie is in the hospital and the honest chain has no natural end. She slipped on the ice; ice is slippery; water expands when it freezes; and a few steps later you are standing at the fundamental nature of electrical forces, unable to answer without rebuilding the world from scratch.

A satisfying answer only exists inside a framework you agree to hold fixed — a set of things you allow to be true. Step outside it and the ground gives way. The trap is explaining the unfamiliar with the familiar when the familiar is secretly powered by the very thing you are trying to explain. Feynman's own example: describing magnets with rubber bands is cheating, because the tension in a rubber band is itself electrical force wearing a disguise.

This why-regress — and how a good question quietly chooses its own frame — is explored from the storytelling side in [The Rules of Story Are the Rules of Being Understood](/post/reflections/rules-of-story-are-the-rules-of-being-understood/).

## Day Science and Night Science

Yanai and Lercher borrow a distinction from François Jacob, who first drew it in his autobiography *The Statue Within*.

Day science is the highroad of progress. It works with fixed targets, solves well-posed puzzles, and fills the gaps in the standing wall of known results. It is the science taught in school: hypothesis, prediction, test, repeat.

Night science is the other half, and the one nobody teaches. It is the unpredictable, wandering process where new ideas are generated and unknown unknowns surface. It advances by an illogical leap into the unknown rather than a careful step. Progress relies on both, but it is night science that produces the questions day science then goes to work on.

## Getting Lost in the Cloud

There is a specific feeling that comes with real work: you have lost the thread, forgotten what you were even trying to do, and you are wandering in confusion. Uri Alon calls this state the cloud — the fog between the goal you set out for and wherever you actually end up.

The instinct is to read the cloud as failure. Alon's point is the reverse. ***The cloud is not a sign that the work has gone wrong. It is a sign that the work is significant.*** The disorientation is the price of leaving the map, and it is inside that fog that a new and better question — a destination you never planned for — tends to appear.

## The Power of Reframing

A real discovery is rarely just an answer. It is usually a solution that gives birth to a new problem. And the breakthroughs seldom come from answering the broad questions a whole field is asking, like "what is the origin of life?" They come from refocusing that sprawl into something small and testable.

CRISPR is the clean example. For years the repeating sequences in bacterial genomes were a curiosity. Francisco Mojica did not ask, in general, why bacteria carried them. He asked something narrower: what does the resemblance between those spacer sequences and known viral DNA tell us about what they do? That reframing turned a curiosity into an immune system.

Good reframings have a strange afterlife. Once a new question rearranges reality, it tends to erase its own origin — the new view feels so obvious that it becomes hard to remember it was ever a leap.

## What to keep

Science is the art of dealing with things we do not know enough about, so uncertainty is the working condition, not an interruption of it.

Check the frame before the answer. When a question refuses to resolve, the problem is often that it needs a deeper level of physics or logic than the current framework allows.

Value the question over the answer. Posing the right one is a credit to a scientist even when the first answer turns out wrong. Hawking's information paradox was the wrong answer to exactly the right question, and it took twenty-five years to settle.
