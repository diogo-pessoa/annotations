## The Premise

Everything has an influence on anything else.

That sentence sounds almost too broad to be useful, and yet it is the quiet starting point of systems thinking. We are trained to explain the world by isolating things: this cause, that effect, this part, that function. It is a tidy habit, and it works often enough that we rarely question it. But most of what we care about — a team, a body, a city, a relationship, a career — does not behave like a collection of separate parts. It behaves like a system.

A system is not just a pile of components. It is components *plus* the relationships between them. Change one relationship and the whole thing shifts, sometimes in ways that have nothing to do with the part you touched.

Much of the vocabulary that follows — feedback loops, delays, leverage points — comes from Donella Meadows's *Thinking in Systems*, still the clearest map of this terrain.

This is the same intuition Brian Klaas reaches from the other direction in [Fluke](/post/reflections/takeaway-from-fluke/): *we control nothing, but we influence everything*. Systems thinking is what that sentence looks like when you take it seriously.

---

## The Whole Is Not the Sum

The first move in systems thinking is to stop asking *what is this thing made of?* and start asking *what is this thing connected to?*

Take any part out of a system and study it on a bench, and you learn something real — but you also lose the very thing that made it interesting. A heart outside a body is a pump. A heart inside a body is part of a loop that includes lungs, blood, stress, sleep, and the argument you had this morning.

The behavior we notice — health, dysfunction, momentum, stagnation — usually lives in the *connections*, not the parts. This is why fixing components in isolation so often fails. The part was never the problem. The relationship was.

---

## Everything Is Feeding Back

Because parts are connected, effects rarely travel in a straight line. They loop.

You act on the system, the system responds, and that response changes the conditions for your next action. Cause and effect stop being a line and become a circle:

1. Something changes
2. The change ripples outward
3. The environment responds
4. That response feeds back into the original change

Two shapes recur:

* **Reinforcing loops** amplify. A small advantage compounds into a large one; a small neglect compounds into decay. Nothing about the loop cares whether the outcome is good — it only makes *more of whatever is already happening*.
* **Balancing loops** stabilize. They resist change and pull the system back toward some set point, which is why so many well-intentioned pushes quietly disappear.

An unwatched reinforcing loop is also how a system grows fragile. Advantage or decay piles up until a single ordinary event tips it over — the avalanche that looks sudden only because no one saw the slope growing.

Nassim Taleb makes the sharper claim across the *Incerto*: fragility scales with size and centralization. A large, tightly coupled system hides its accumulated risk and then breaks all at once, while smaller, independent units fail small, fail often, and stay resilient — even gaining from the shocks that would topple something bigger.

Most of the frustration of trying to change anything real comes from misreading which loop you are standing in.

---

## Delay Is Where Intuition Breaks

The influence between parts is real, but it is rarely instant. There is almost always a delay between a cause and the effect we can see.

Delay is what makes systems so hard to reason about. We push, nothing seems to happen, so we push harder — and then the delayed effect of *both* pushes arrives at once and overshoots. The problem was never the size of our effort. It was our impatience with the lag.

Delay is also why we [misjudge risk so badly](/post/reflections/unthinkable-risk-assessments-risk-aversion/). The danger accumulates during the lag, out of sight, and the eventual failure looks like a bolt from nowhere — the blind spot Brian Klaas calls *non-stationarity*, [the ground shifting while you measure it](/post/reflections/takeaway-from-fluke/). A sand pile grows tall during exactly the interval we are too impatient to watch.

Learning to respect delay — to act, then wait long enough to actually observe — is one of the least glamorous and most valuable systems habits.

---

## Where to Push: Leverage

If everything influences everything else, the obvious worry is paralysis: where do you even intervene?

The answer is that not all points in a system are equal. Some are surface-level — you can push on them all day and the system absorbs it. Others are deep, and a small nudge there reorganizes everything downstream. This ordering, from shallow to deep, is the heart of Meadows's *Leverage Points*.

Weak leverage tends to be at the level of **events and numbers**: symptoms, quotas, quick fixes. Strong leverage tends to be at the level of **structure and assumptions**: the way parts are connected, the goals the system is optimizing for, the beliefs nobody questions.

Sometimes the highest-leverage move is simply drawing a boundary in the right place. One well-placed interface reorganizes everything that connects through it, while a thousand surface fixes never touch the tangle underneath.

The uncomfortable part is that the highest-leverage interventions are usually the least visible and the hardest to act on. It is far easier to treat a symptom than to change the assumption that keeps producing it.

---

## Coherence as a Stabilizer

There is a deeper reason those interventions are so hard: the system pushes back. And it does not resist change out of stubbornness. Resistance is often a sign that the current structure is doing something — holding things together, reducing uncertainty, keeping the system functional under stress.

This is worth remembering before rushing to "fix" anything. A structure that looks irrational from the outside may be a load-bearing wall on the inside. Challenging it can feel disruptive not because it is wrong, but because it is *holding weight*. Understanding what a structure stabilizes is a precondition for changing it safely.

---

## A Different Set of Questions

Once you assume interconnection, the questions change.

Instead of *"what happened?"* and *"whose fault is it?"*, more useful questions become:

* *What is this connected to that I'm not looking at?*
* *What loop am I standing inside?*
* *Is this a reinforcing or a balancing dynamic?*
* *Where is the delay hiding?*
* *What is the smallest change at the deepest level?*

None of these have quick answers. That is the point. Systems thinking trades the comfort of clean explanations for the accuracy of messy ones.

---

## Closing Reflection

Viewing the whole as a system is less a technique than a posture. It is the willingness to hold the whole in view even when the parts are begging for isolated attention.

The claim that *everything influences everything else* is not an invitation to feel overwhelmed. It is an invitation to look for the connections we habitually ignore — the loop we are inside, the delay we are impatient with, the assumption we never questioned — and to notice that the highest leverage is almost never where the loudest symptom is.

The parts will always ask to be fixed one at a time. The system rarely works that way.
